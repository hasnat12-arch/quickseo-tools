Why Word Count Still Matters for SEO in 2026

Guide

There's no magic word count that guarantees a page will rank. Google has said many times that length by itself is not a ranking factor. What word count actually reflects is depth: a 300-word page rarely has room to answer a reader's question fully, while a 1,200-word page usually does — not because it's longer, but because it had space to cover related questions, examples, and edge cases.

A useful habit before publishing is to check your draft against the top few results already ranking for your target keyword. If they all sit around 1,000-1,500 words and cover five sub-topics, a 400-word post touching only one of those sub-topics is unlikely to compete, regardless of how well it's written.

Use a word counter as a sanity check, not a target. Write until the reader's question is fully answered, then stop — padding a thin answer to hit a number usually hurts readability more than it helps rankings.

Keyword Density: How Much Repetition Is Too Much?

Guide

Keyword density — the percentage of times a word or phrase appears in your text — used to be a bigger deal than it is today. Older SEO advice suggested hitting a specific percentage (often 1-2%). Modern search engines are far better at understanding synonyms and context, so chasing an exact percentage is no longer necessary and can backfire.

The real risk today is keyword stuffing: repeating the same phrase so often that the writing reads unnaturally. If your main keyword shows up in every second sentence, that's a signal to rewrite, not a signal you're doing SEO correctly.

A better approach: write naturally for the reader first, then run your draft through a keyword density checker afterward. If one word dominates the results at a much higher percentage than everything else, that's worth a second look — not because of a fixed threshold, but because it usually means the writing has gotten repetitive.

Meta Titles & Descriptions: The 60/155 Rule Explained

Guide

Google's search results display a limited amount of space for each result's title and description snippet. In practice, titles beyond roughly 60 characters and descriptions beyond roughly 155 characters usually get cut off with "...", which can make a result look unfinished or hide your call to action entirely.

These aren't hard technical limits — Google measures by pixel width, not character count, so a title full of narrow letters (like "i" and "l") can run a bit longer before truncating than one full of wide letters (like "m" and "w"). Character counts are still the easiest practical guideline to work with while drafting.

A simple workflow: write your title and description as you normally would, then check the length before publishing. If it's close to the limit, put your most important keyword and the strongest reason to click near the start — that way, even if the end gets cut off, the part someone actually reads still does its job.

Why Clean URL Slugs Are Worth the Extra Minute

Guide

Left on autopilot, most content management systems will turn a post title like "10 Best Free SEO Tools in 2026!" into something like 10-best-free-seo-tools-in-2026 — which is fine — or occasionally into a messy string with numbers, underscores, or leftover punctuation, which is not.

A clean slug matters for two small but real reasons. First, readers glance at URLs before clicking — in shared links, browser tabs, and search results — and a tidy, readable slug looks more trustworthy than a jumbled one. Second, while URL structure is a minor ranking signal at best, a slug that clearly matches your title and target keyword removes any doubt about what the page is about.

The rule of thumb: lowercase letters, words separated by hyphens (not underscores), no stop words like "the" or "and" unless they change the meaning, and no dates or numbers that will make the URL look stale a year from now unless the content is genuinely time-specific.
